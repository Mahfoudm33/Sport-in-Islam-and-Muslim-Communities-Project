Between Globalization and Local 'Modernity': The Diffusion and Modernization of Football in Algeria
Soccer & Society, Vol. 5, No. 1 (2004), pp. 1–26 · Routledge / Taylor & Francis
#Algeria#football #political-Islam#FLN #one-party-state#multiparty-transition #postcolonialism#nation-building

Co-authored with Professor Ian P. Henry (Loughborough University), this article traces the diffusion and transformation of football in Algeria from the colonial period through to the post-socialist market economy era — examining how football's meaning and function shifted at each political conjuncture. The paper's central analytical contribution is a periodisation framework (later reproduced as Table 23.1 in the Houlihan Sport and Society textbook) mapping the changing role of sport across five phases of Algerian history:

1926–57 (Colonialism): Sport initially organised around nationalist and European groups; football progressively appropriated by the nationalist movement as an instrument of anti-colonial subversion and political expression.

1954–62 (Revolution): The FLN football team (1958) used the sport to internationalise the Algerian cause for independence — one of the most historically significant uses of sport diplomacy in the Global South.

1962–88 (FLN single-party state): Football deployed as a tool of nation-state building, socialist legitimation, and pan-African solidarity. Era of enforced amateurism.

1988–92 (Economic crisis and transition): Football stadiums become arenas of political agitation, social protest, and rejection of inequalities — coinciding with Algeria's opening toward multipartyism and, critically, the rise of political Islam (the FIS and the broader Islamist movement). The paper examines how sport became a contested terrain between secular state nationalism and political Islam during this turbulent transition period.

From 1992 (Market economy): Commercial sport emerges in a locally specific Algerian form, shaped by the legacy of the Black Decade, globalisation pressures, and ongoing ideological tensions.

Why it matters This is the foundational empirical paper of the research programme — the work that established the author's comparative framework for understanding sport in postcolonial, Muslim-majority societies navigating between global sporting norms and local political and religious identities. Its periodisation table has been reproduced and cited across multiple subsequent works and textbooks. It is one of the earliest academic analyses to situate political Islam directly within the sociology of sport, treating the transition from one-party to multiparty state as a key variable in understanding football's contested meanings in North Africa.

Sage · Sport and Society Textbook
In Houlihan (ed.), Sport and Society: A Student Introduction (2nd ed.), Sage · pp. 532–552
#Islam#Muslim-world #comparative#Shari'a#Ijtihad

This foundational chapter distinguishes between two types of inquiry: (a) sport in Islam — examining modern sport through the lens of Islamic jurisprudence and the Qur'an/Sunnah; and (b) sport in Muslim societies — a sociological and postcolonial examination of sport across heterogeneous Muslim nation-states. The chapter provides non-Muslim readers with an accessible entry point to Islamic ontology (Tawhid), sources of jurisprudence, and the five pillars, before addressing how Muslim scholars debate sport's permissibility. It surveys the diversity of Muslim states from revolutionary-modernist (Tunisia, Algeria, Turkey) to conservative theocratic (Iran, Afghanistan) to constitutional monarchies (Qatar, Saudi Arabia).

Why it matters Published in one of the most widely adopted sport studies textbooks, this chapter has introduced thousands of students globally to sport in Muslim contexts. It remains a standard course reading in sport sociology and management programmes worldwide.

Brown Journal of World Affairs
Brown Journal of World Affairs, Vol. XIV, Issue 2, pp. 67–75 (Spring/Summer 2008)
#Muslim-world#postcolonialism #GANEFO#Islamic-Games#gender

Published in Brown University's prestigious policy journal, this article investigates how modern sport — as a product of Western modernity — both shapes and reflects transformations in the Muslim world. Three illustrative case studies frame the argument: (1) sport and nation-state building in the postcolonial era, from GANEFO (1963) to Gulf commercialisation; (2) the condition of women in Muslim societies through the lens of Muslim women's Olympic participation; and (3) the inaugural 2005 Islamic Solidarity Games as a reassertion of Islamic identity in global sport. The article argues that the Muslim world's engagement with sport is not a simple story of Westernisation, but a complex negotiation between global and local, modernity and authenticity.

Why it matters One of the few academic analyses of sport in the Muslim world published in an international affairs journal — bridging sport studies with political science and area studies audiences. It positions sport as a domain of geopolitical significance, not merely cultural practice.

Int. Journal of the History of Sport · Routledge
International Journal of the History of Sport, Vol. 29, No. 4, pp. 638–651 · DOI: 10.1080/09523367.2012.658194
#Beijing2008#hijab #gender#Olympics #media-analysis#Orientalism

This paper undertakes a comparative analysis of international media accounts — Western (English, French), Arab (Arabic), Iranian, and Chinese — regarding approximately 14 veiled Muslim athletes who competed in Beijing 2008. Using inductive thematic analysis, the paper identifies four dominant discourses: (1) Western secular media framing the veil as incompatible with Olympic values; (2) Iranian and Arab media celebrating veiled athletes as evidence of Muslim women's agency; (3) the "commercialised hijab" debate generated by Roqaya Al-Ghasara's Australian-designed "hijood"; (4) Chinese media using the Games as a narrative of civilisational bridge-building. The paper critically deploys Orientalism theory to challenge the Western media's condescension toward Muslim women's autonomous choices.

Why it matters A methodologically distinctive work — one of the few comparative multilingual media analyses in sport studies. It challenges both Western secular universalism and Islamist restrictions on women's bodies, positioning the author as a genuinely cross-cultural scholarly voice.

Religions · MDPI · Open Access
Religions (MDPI), Vol. 4, No. 4, pp. 644–656 · Published 10 December 2013 · DOI: 10.3390/rel4040644
#Islam#Europe #France#identity #laïcité#reverse-migration #Orientalism#post-secular

Using Arkoun's concept of "the crisis of meanings," this article examines how misconceptions about Islam's role in Europe distort the discourse on sport, immigration, and integration. France serves as the principal case study — the country where debates on secularism (laïcité), Muslim identity, and republican values are most contentious. The paper is structured across three analytical layers: (1) the religious phenomenon in Europe, drawing on Touraine's distinction between communautarisme (self-segregation) and legitimate "cultural rights"; Habermas's notion of the "post-secular" society; and Arkoun's critique of the Western "culture of disbelief"; (2) a typology of Muslim identity positions in Europe — from Ritualised and Nationalist Islam to Neo-reformist and Secular — providing a sophisticated framework for understanding Muslim heterogeneity; and (3) a series of sport incidents in France used as prisms for the "crisis of meanings": Zidane's headbutt at the 2006 World Cup (read through Orientalist frames of "irrationality" and "Muslim masculinity"); the booing of La Marseillaise at the France–Tunisia friendly (2008); and the halal meals controversy during the 2010 World Cup. The paper also analyses the "reverse migration" phenomenon — North African-origin athletes in Europe choosing to represent Algeria, Tunisia, or Morocco — as a terrain of contested loyalties on both sides of the Mediterranean.

Why it matters One of the few academic papers to apply a theoretically sophisticated European sociology of religion framework (Habermas, Touraine, Arkoun, Said) directly to the sport domain. It moves beyond both assimilationist and multiculturalist frameworks to show how sport functions as a politicised space where Muslim athletes are held to impossible double standards of loyalty — in their countries of birth and their countries of origin simultaneously. The Zidane, Marseillaise, and halal incidents remain touchstone cases in the sociology of sport and Islam in Europe.

Palgrave Macmillan · Islam in the West
In Farrar, Robinson, Vallic & Wetherly (eds.), Islam in the West: Key Issues in Multiculturalism, Palgrave Macmillan (2012), pp. 138–153
#Muslim-minorities#sport-welfare #UK#multiculturalism #Leicester#Birmingham

Co-authored with Professor Ian P. Henry (Loughborough University), this chapter appears in a Palgrave volume on Islam and multiculturalism in the West. It critically deconstructs the framing of Muslim communities in British sport policy and welfare discourse, drawing on empirical case studies from Leicester and Birmingham — two cities with significant Muslim populations. The chapter challenges reductive representations of Muslim sporting needs as a "problem" to be managed, arguing instead for a framework that recognises the complex, heterogeneous, and locally specific character of Muslim communities' relationship with sport, leisure, and public services. It connects to the broader chapter's wider argument about sport as a site where the integration debate is tested and contested.

Why it matters Brings together the theoretical framework developed in the Religions 2013 article with empirical evidence from British Muslim communities, contributing directly to sport welfare and sport policy debates in a multicultural European context. It is a key reference for practitioners and researchers working on inclusion, diversity, and sport provision for minority communities.

Routledge · Sport in Islam
In Testa & Amara (eds.), Sport in Islam and in Muslim Communities, Routledge (2016) · pp. [Chapter 7]
#Tunisia#Arab-Spring #Ennahda#London2012#Islamism

Written in the wake of the 2011 Arab Spring, this chapter examines sport as a contested terrain during Tunisia's political transition under the Islamist party Ennahda (2012–2014). The author analyses the 2012 London Olympics as a test case: gold medallist Oussama El-Melouli was targeted by a Salafi Jihadi group for drinking water during Ramadan; silver medallist Habiba Ghribi faced calls to have her citizenship stripped for her "indecent" athletic attire. The chapter traces how Tunisia's secular-nationalist sport heritage, built under Bourguiba and Ben Ali, became a battlefield between Islamist and secular movements — with the bodies of female athletes as the central site of contestation.

Why it matters A rare primary-source analysis of the intersection of sport, religion, and post-revolutionary politics in North Africa. The chapter demonstrates how sport functions as a barometer of political transition — and how women athletes' bodies become symbolic battlegrounds in ideological struggles.

Routledge · Edited Volume
Routledge, London (2016) · Series: Routledge Research in Sport, Culture and Society, No. 50
#edited-volume#Islam #Muslim-communities#comparative#Routledge

This edited volume brings together 14 contributions from scholars across sport sociology, anthropology, Islamic studies, and political science to examine sport in Muslim-majority countries and among Muslim minorities in the West. Structured across four parts — Understanding Sport in Islam; Gender, Body and Culture; Sport and Politics of Identity; Sport Development and Sport for Development — the volume offers the most comprehensive comparative treatment of its subject to date. Chapters cover Germany, Norway, Turkey, Palestine, Tunisia, Malaysia, the UK, and Italy. Amara co-authored the Introduction and Conclusion, and contributed the Tunisia chapter.

Why it matters The first systematic comparative edited volume on sport and Islam across both Muslim-majority and Muslim-minority contexts. Published by the world's leading sport studies publisher, it became a standard reference in the field and is widely assigned in graduate courses in sport sociology, Islamic studies, and migration studies.
2016
Routledge · Sport Marketing
In Testa & Amara (eds.), Sport in Islam and in Muslim Communities, Routledge (2016)
#halal-marketing#sport-marketing #consumerism#Gulf#Qatar

This chapter examines the emerging field of "Islamic marketing" in relation to sport, interrogating whether marketing — rooted in free-market capitalism and consumerism — can be genuinely reconciled with Islamic ethics. The chapter surveys halal/haram distinctions in sport sponsorship (alcohol, gambling, usury), analyses case studies including Frederic Kanoute's refusal to wear a gambling sponsor's shirt, Papiss Cissé's dispute with Newcastle FC over the Wonga logo, and Qatar's dilemma over beer sponsorship at the 2022 FIFA World Cup. It also examines the growing halal beer market as a paradox of Islamic consumerism.

Why it matters Provides the first theoretical framework for understanding sport marketing in Muslim contexts — a gap that became commercially urgent as Gulf states invested billions in global sport and as the Muslim consumer market reached 1.8 billion people. Directly relevant to practitioners in sport management and brand strategy.

Qatar University · Football & Faith
Mahfoud Amara & Youcef Bouandel · Qatar University · 2022
#Qatar2022#Islam #football#foi #méga-événement#sécularisation #joueurs-musulmans

Cet article examine la Coupe du monde 2022 de la FIFA au Qatar comme un prisme révélateur des tensions entre sport, religion, modernité et identité. Structuré en trois parties, il traite d'abord de la manière dont le Qatar — en tant que pays arabe et majoritairement musulman accueillant pour la première fois la Coupe du monde — a dû naviguer entre les exigences commerciales et organisationnelles du tournoi (alcool, droits LGBT+, culture des supporters) et les valeurs islamiques locales. La deuxième partie analyse la participation des pays à majorité musulmane (Maroc, Tunisie, Arabie saoudite, Iran) et interroge ce que cela signifie dans le contexte des débats sur l'islam et la sécularisation. La troisième partie se penche sur les joueurs professionnels de culture et d'origine musulmane — tels que Mohamed Salah, Sadio Mané et Achraf Hakimi — qui doivent naviguer entre leur identité religieuse, les attentes de leurs clubs et sponsors, et les pressions des médias sociaux. L'article ouvre également sur la question plus large du football comme quasi-religion : de l'Iglesia Maradoniana à la vénération des stades, le football partage avec la religion des rituels, des sanctuaires et une communauté de croyants.

Pourquoi ce travail est important Première analyse académique de la Coupe du monde 2022 sous l'angle de l'islam et de la foi, co-produite depuis le Qatar pendant l'événement lui-même. L'article articule les débats sur la gouvernance mondiale du sport, les droits culturels, l'islamophobie, et la diplomatie sportive des États du Golfe — des enjeux directement liés à la position géographique et institutionnelle de l'auteur à Qatar University.
